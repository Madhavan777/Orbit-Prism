The problem and our answer

A satellite sees far more than it can send.

Problem statement

An Earth-observation satellite captures gigabytes of imagery, but a ten-minute ground-station pass over a 256 kbit/s link carries only 19.2 MB, and the chip that thinks has just 4.2 MB of working memory.

Much of that link is wasted on cloudy frames, empty frames, and urgent tiles that wait for the next pass. The task: decide, on board and within those limits, what to send first, and say honestly how many important events might be missed.

  • Link: 256 kbit/s × 600 s = 19.2 MB per pass
  • Memory: 4.2 MB, so a full 6.8 MB chip must be cut into tiles
  • Worked example: floods, using real Sentinel-1 radar and Sentinel-2 optical imagery

Our solution: ORBIT-PRISM

Cut each image into 16 tiles, score every tile with a tiny INT8 network, and send in order of value per byte, in three layers: a 32-byte alert, a 2 KiB preview, then the full tile.

  • Miss-rate dial. The operator picks the share of critical tiles they accept missing (1%, 5% or 10%); a calibrated cut-off per sensor delivers it, and we report what was actually achieved on a flood the models never saw.
  • Radar fallback. When cloud covers half a tile or more, the radar model takes over, because radar sees through cloud. A cloud filter alone would discard the flood.
  • Byte-budget scheduler. Tiles are ranked by flood probability × extent per byte until the pass is spent; the rest carry over.
